How I lead

Three cases on growing people, earning trust, and giving design a real seat in roadmap decisions.

I’m Brian, a Design Manager at PayPay. Over 11 years I’ve led five design teams at Wego and PayPay, each with three to nine designers.

  1. A Growing designers
  2. B Stakeholder trust

Step01

Growing designers who were stuck

Outcome

Within a year, both had been promoted. They needed very different things from me, and working out which was which was most of the job.

A Confidence

The problem

He joined my team after years of low ratings. He agreed to every request, rarely pushed back, and waited to be told what to do. Fixing his files wouldn’t have helped. What he was missing was the confidence to act on his own, and to defend his thinking.

What I did

  1. I gave him one of his own ideas to own from start to finish, including planning what came next, and made it clear I’d back him if it went wrong.
  2. I told him plainly that mistakes are the path to wisdom.
  3. Speaking up felt risky. He usually brought ideas without evidence, so any question felt like an attack. Together we wrote a one-page method we called a strong why: pros, cons, and the data or research behind them.

The result

He could question stakeholders, and push back when he thought they were wrong. Within about six months he had a top rating and a promotion.

B Influence

The problem

He had plenty of ideas. The problem was follow-through. He’d raise a proposal, it wouldn’t go anywhere, and he couldn’t describe what “senior” actually required.

What I did

  1. We worked on how trust gets built: finish what you start, present it clearly, and show that serious work is safe with you.
  2. I coached him on nemawashi: aligning people in small conversations before the formal meeting.
  3. We broke his goal into smaller projects, listed his strengths and sticking points and worked through them, and wrote a plain description of the senior role so he knew what he was aiming for.

The result

Once his ideas started landing, people started backing them. He reached senior in about six months, and lead designer in about a year.

Step02

Earning stakeholder trust

Outcome

At Wego, the head of product, engineering, and the C-suite adopted a new design process. My first attempt failed. A real result did what the pitch couldn’t.

The problem

When I joined, managing six designers, there was almost no process. A request came in and a design file went out. No quality checks, peer review, design system, or user testing. The head of product approved everything herself.

What I did

  1. Soon after I started, I told her this needed to change. I was right about the gaps, but she had no reason yet to trust my judgment, and it went nowhere.
  2. I asked the designers what they’d fix. Together we listed the problems, possible solutions, and priorities.
  3. We tested the new process on one live project, without waiting for formal approval. The core change was layered design reviews: designers reviewed each other’s work in rounds, and engineers joined early, so constraints surfaced while changes were still cheap. She only needed a single final review.
  4. The project succeeded. We walked the wider team through what we’d changed, and why.

The result

  • A faster, more efficient process.
  • The head of product no longer joined every design review, only the final one.
  • Engineers were involved early, avoiding wasted design effort.
  • User research and interviews started, validating design decisions.
  • She came on board, then engineering, then the C-suite.

At PayPay I saw similar patterns, so I started with questions instead of instructions, first with my product manager and then with the designers. We built the plan together and proved it on live projects, and the head of product took notice. Today, people know that when I take something on, I see it through.

Step03

Giving design a voice on the roadmap

Outcome

My designers now help decide what gets built, not just how it looks. They question briefs before designing, get proposals onto the roadmap every season, and in one case stopped a project users didn’t need, saving hundreds of hours across product, engineering, and design.

The problem

It didn’t start that way. A product manager would send a brief, a designer would design it, take feedback, and ship. Nobody asked what the work was for, and when scope grew, the team absorbed it.

They cared about quality, but they were afraid to say no, to speak up in meetings, or to look unprepared. Some felt they didn’t know enough to have an opinion. A team that can’t turn down the wrong work never has time for the right work.

What I did

  1. I made saying no a normal part of the job, and I did it visibly. I presented to the whole team on when no is the right answer, practiced it with each designer one on one, and stepped in on live projects to stop scope creep.
  2. I made it safer to speak up. If you have data, lead with it. If you don’t yet, share the idea anyway and be upfront: I’m assuming here, and I don’t know the right answer yet. Not having the answer is fine. That’s what the team is for.
  3. They learned nemawashi (aligning people before the formal meeting) by joining me in those conversations until they could run them on their own.

The result

Briefs get questions before they get designs: what’s this for, what does success look like, who is it for, and which business metric should move?

  • Designers bring proposals to roadmap planning, backed by real problems and data. A few are accepted every season.
  • They start their own initiatives. One designer built a plugin so marketing, business, and design create illustrations from the same asset library.

The clearest example is a cancelled project. Given a brief, the designer interviewed users before designing anything, found there was no real need, and made the case to stop. The best outcome for that brief was not building it.
