How I lead

Three cases on growing people, earning trust, and a seat in the roadmap.

I’m Brian, a Design Manager at PayPay. Over 11 years I’ve led five design teams at Wego and PayPay, three to nine designers each.

Case

Growing designers who were stuck

Outcome

Within a year, both had been promoted. They needed different things from me, and telling those apart was most of the job.

Confidence

The problem

He joined after years of low ratings. He agreed to every request, rarely pushed back, and waited to be told what to do. Fixing his files would not have helped. He needed the confidence to act, and to defend his thinking.

What I did

  1. I gave him one of his own ideas to own from start to finish, including what came next, and said I’d back him if it went wrong.
  2. I told him that mistakes are the path to wisdom.
  3. He brought ideas without evidence, so questions felt like attacks. We wrote a one-page “strong why”: pros, cons, and the data or research behind them.

The result

He could question stakeholders and push back. Within about six months he had a top rating and a promotion.

Influence

The problem

He had plenty of ideas. Follow-through was the gap. A proposal would go nowhere, and he could not describe what senior required.

What I did

  1. Trust is built by finishing what you start, presenting it clearly, and showing that serious work is safe with you.
  2. I coached him on nemawashi: aligning people in small conversations before the formal meeting.
  3. We broke the goal into smaller projects, worked through strengths and sticking points, and wrote a plain description of the senior role.

The result

His ideas started landing, and people backed them. Senior in about six months. Lead designer in about a year.

Case

Earning stakeholder trust

Outcome

At Wego, the head of product, engineering, and the C-suite adopted a new design process. My first attempt failed. A real result did what the pitch could not.

The problem

Managing six designers, there was almost no process. A request came in and a file went out. No quality checks, peer review, design system, or user testing. The head of product approved everything herself.

What I did

  1. I told her this needed to change. I was right about the gaps, but she had no reason yet to trust my judgment, and it went nowhere.
  2. I asked the designers what they would fix. Together we listed the problems, possible solutions, and priorities.
  3. We tested it on one live project, without waiting for approval. Layered reviews: designers in rounds, engineers in early, one final review for her.
  4. The project succeeded. We walked the wider team through what had changed, and why.

The result

  • A faster, more efficient process.
  • She joined only the final review.
  • Engineers were in early, so less effort was wasted.
  • User research and interviews started.
  • Then she, engineering, and the C-suite.

At PayPay I started with questions, not instructions. First my product manager, then the designers. We proved it on live projects. The head of product took notice. When I take something on, I see it through.

Case

Giving design a voice on the roadmap

Outcome

Designers now help decide what gets built, not just how it looks. They question briefs first, land proposals every season, and once stopped a project users didn’t need, saving hundreds of hours across product, engineering, and design.

The problem

A brief arrived, a designer designed it, took feedback, and shipped. Nobody asked what the work was for. When scope grew, the team absorbed it. They were afraid to say no, to speak up, or to look unprepared. A team that can’t turn down the wrong work never has time for the right work.

What I did

  1. I made saying no normal, and visible. I talked to the whole team, practiced it with each designer, and stepped in when scope kept growing.
  2. If you have data, lead with it. If you don’t, say so: “I’m assuming here, and I don’t know the right answer yet.” That is what the team is for.
  3. They learned nemawashi, aligning before the formal meeting, by joining me until they could run those conversations alone.

The result

Briefs get questions first: what is this for, what does success look like, who is it for, which metric should move. A few proposals, backed by real problems and data, are accepted every season.

One designer built a plugin so marketing, business, and design draw from the same illustration library. Another interviewed users before designing, found no real need, and made the case to stop. The best outcome was not building it.

Brian (Briandito Priambodo), Design Manager.
