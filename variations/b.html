  1. 1

    I’m Brian, a Design Manager F at PayPay E. Over 11 years A I’ve led five teams B, three to nine designers C each, at Wego D and PayPay.

  3. 2

    Outcome. Both promoted within a year. They needed different things. Telling those apart was the job.

  4. 3

    Years of low ratings. He agreed to every request, rarely pushed back, and waited to be told. Not a file problem. He needed confidence to act, and to defend his thinking.

  5. 4

    Give him one of his own ideas to own, including what comes next. Back him if it goes wrong. Tell him mistakes are the path to wisdom.

  6. 5

    He brought ideas with no evidence, so questions felt like attacks. Write a “strong why”: pros, cons, and the data or research. He could push back. Top rating and a promotion in about six months.

  8. 6

    Plenty of ideas, little follow-through. A proposal would go nowhere. He could not describe senior. Finish the work, present it clearly, show that serious work is safe with you.

  9. 7

    Nemawashi: align people in small conversations before the formal meeting. Smaller projects. Strengths, sticking points, and a plain description of the senior role.

  10. 8

    Result. Ideas landed. People backed them. Senior in about six months. Lead designer in about a year.

  11. 9

    Outcome. At Wego D, the head of product, engineering, and the C-suite adopted the process. The first pitch failed. A real result did what it could not.

  12. 10

    Six designers. A request in, a file out. No quality checks, peer review, design system, or user testing. She approved everything.

  13. 11

    I was right about the gaps. She had no reason to trust me yet. It went nowhere. Ask the designers. List problems, solutions, and priorities.

  14. 12

    Test on one live project, without formal approval. Layered reviews: designers in rounds, engineers early while changes are cheap, one final review for her. Then walk the team through it.

  15. 13

    Result. Faster process. She joins only the final review. Engineers early, less wasted effort. User research starts. Then she, engineering, and the C-suite.

  17. 14

    At PayPay E, the same pattern. Questions, not instructions. Product manager first, then the designers. Build the plan together and prove it on live projects. The head of product notices. When I take something on, I see it through.

  18. 15

    Outcome. Designers help decide what gets built, not just how it looks. They question briefs, land proposals every season, and stopped a project users didn’t need. Hundreds of hours saved across product, engineering, and design.

  19. 16

    A brief was designed, reviewed, and shipped. Nobody asked what it was for. Scope was absorbed. They were afraid to say no, to speak up, or to look unprepared. Some felt they did not know enough. Wrong work leaves no time for the right work.

  20. 17

    Make saying no normal, and visible. Talk to the team. Practice with each designer. Step in when scope grows. Lead with data, or say so: “I’m assuming here, and I don’t know the right answer yet.” That is what the team is for.

  21. 18

    Nemawashi again: they join the pre-meeting conversations until they can run them. Briefs get questions first. Purpose, success, who, which metric.

  22. 19

    Proposals backed by real problems and data. A few accepted every season. One plugin so marketing, business, and design share an illustration library.

  23. 20

    Given a brief, the designer interviewed users before designing, found no real need, and made the case to stop. The best outcome was not building it.
