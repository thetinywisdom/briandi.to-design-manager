How I lead

I’m Brian, a Design Manager at PayPay. Three cases: growing people, earning trust, and a seat in the roadmap.

The sheet

Design Manager at PayPay. 11 years. Five teams of three to nine designers, at Wego and PayPay. The rest of the facts sit in the title block.

Read this way

Each figure is a case. The note beside it is the outcome first, then the move that got us there.

Outcome

Within a year, both had been promoted. They needed different things, and telling those apart was most of the job.

Confidence

Years of low ratings. He agreed to every request, rarely pushed back, and waited to be told. Fixing his files would not have helped. I gave him one of his own ideas to own, including what came next, and said I’d back him. Mistakes are the path to wisdom. A “strong why” — pros, cons, and the research — let him question stakeholders and push back. Top rating and a promotion in about six months.

Influence

Plenty of ideas. Follow-through was the gap. A proposal would go nowhere, and he could not describe what senior required. Trust is built by finishing what you start, presenting it clearly, and showing that serious work is safe with you. Nemawashi means aligning people in small conversations before the formal meeting. We broke the goal into smaller projects, worked through strengths and sticking points, and wrote a plain description of the senior role. His ideas started landing, and people backed them. Senior in about six months. Lead designer in about a year.

Outcome

At Wego, the head of product, engineering, and the C-suite adopted the process. My pitch failed. A real result did what it could not.

The gap

Six designers. A request in, a file out. No quality checks, peer review, design system, or user testing. She approved every step. I was right about the gaps, and she had no reason to trust my judgment yet, so it went nowhere.

The move

I asked the designers what they would fix. We listed problems, solutions, and priorities, then tested layered reviews on one live project without waiting for approval. Designers in rounds, engineers in early while changes were still cheap, one final review for her. The project succeeded. We walked the wider team through what had changed. She joined only the final review. Faster work, less wasted effort, and user research started. Then she, engineering, and the C-suite.

At PayPay

At PayPay, the same pattern. Questions, not instructions. First my product manager, then the designers. We built the plan together and proved it on live projects. The head of product took notice. When I take something on, I see it through.

Outcome

Designers help decide what gets built, not just how it looks. They question briefs, land proposals every season, and once stopped a project users didn’t need, saving hundreds of hours across product, engineering, and design.

Before

A brief arrived, a designer designed it, took feedback, and shipped. Nobody asked what the work was for. When scope grew, the team absorbed it. They cared about quality, and they were afraid to say no, to speak up, or to look unprepared. Some felt they did not know enough. A team that can’t turn down the wrong work never has time for the right work.

The practice

I made saying no normal, and visible: a talk to the team, practice with each designer, and stepping in on scope creep. Lead with data, or say so: “I’m assuming here, and I don’t know the right answer yet.” That is what the team is for. Nemawashi: they join the pre-meeting conversations until they can run them.

Now

Briefs get questions first: what is this for, what does success look like, who is it for, which metric should move. Proposals backed by real problems and data, a few accepted every season. One plugin so marketing, business, and design share an illustration library. One designer interviewed users before designing, found no real need, and made the case to stop. The best outcome was not building it.
