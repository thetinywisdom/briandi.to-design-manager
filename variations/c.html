How I lead

Brian, Design Manager. Notes on people, trust, and the roadmap.

Role

01

Growing designers who were stuck

01

Within a year, both had been promoted. They needed different things, and telling those apart was most of the job.

02

He joined after years of low ratings. He agreed to every request, rarely pushed back, and waited to be told what to do. Fixing his files would not have helped. He needed the confidence to act, and to defend his thinking.

03

I gave him one of his own ideas to own from start to finish, including what came next, and said I’d back him if it went wrong. I told him that mistakes are the path to wisdom.

04

Ideas without evidence made questions feel like attacks. We wrote a “strong why”: pros, cons, and the research. He could question stakeholders and push back. Top rating and a promotion in about six months.

05

He had plenty of ideas. Follow-through was the gap. A proposal would go nowhere, and he could not describe what senior required. Trust is built by finishing what you start, presenting it clearly, and showing that serious work is safe with you.

06

Nemawashi: align people in small conversations before the formal meeting. We worked through strengths and sticking points, and wrote a plain description of the senior role. People backed the work. Senior in about six months, lead designer in about a year.

02

Earning stakeholder trust

01

At Wego, the head of product, engineering, and the C-suite adopted the process. My pitch failed. A real result did what it could not.

02

Managing six designers, there was almost no process. A request came in and a file went out. No quality checks, peer review, design system, or user testing. The head of product approved every step herself.

03

I was right about the gaps, and she had no reason to trust me yet, so it went nowhere. We listed problems, solutions, and priorities with the designers, then tested one live project without waiting for approval.

04

Layered reviews: designers in rounds, engineers in early while changes were still cheap, one final review for her. The project succeeded. We walked the wider team through what had changed. She joined only the final review. Faster work, less wasted effort, and user research started. Then she, engineering, and the C-suite.

05

At PayPay I started with questions, not instructions. Product manager first, then the designers. We built the plan together and proved it on live projects. The head of product took notice. People know I see things through.

03

Giving design a voice on the roadmap

01

Designers help decide what gets built, not just how it looks. They question briefs, land proposals every season, and once stopped a project users didn’t need, saving hundreds of hours across product, engineering, and design.

02

A brief arrived, a designer designed it, took feedback, and shipped. Nobody asked what the work was for. When scope grew, the team absorbed it. They were afraid to say no, to speak up, or to look unprepared. Some felt they did not know enough. A team that can’t turn down the wrong work never has time for the right work.

03

I made saying no normal, and visible: a talk, practice with each designer, and stepping in on scope creep. Lead with data, or say so: “I’m assuming here, and I don’t know the right answer yet.” That is what the team is for.

04

They learned nemawashi, aligning before the formal meeting, by joining me until they could run those conversations alone. Briefs get questions first: what is this for, what does success look like, who is it for, which metric should move. A few proposals, backed by real problems and data, are accepted every season.

05

One designer built a plugin so marketing, business, and design draw from the same illustration library. Another was given a brief, interviewed users before designing anything, found no real need, and made the case to stop. The best outcome was not building it.
